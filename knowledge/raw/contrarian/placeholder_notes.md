title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
author: Kahneman
year: 2011
doc_type: book
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. System 1 is fast and intuitive, System 2 slow and deliberate. Biases include anchoring, availability, representativeness, overconfidence, loss aversion and the narrative fallacy (WYSIATI: what you see is all there is). Experts in low-validity environments such as stock picking are prone to an illusion of skill.

---
title: Irrational Exuberance
author: Shiller
year: 2000
doc_type: book
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Shiller argues that asset bubbles are driven by feedback loops: rising prices attract attention and stories, which draw in more buyers and push prices further. Precipitating factors (new technology, media expansion, cheap credit, the rise of retail trading) and amplification mechanisms (naturally occurring Ponzi processes, new-era thinking, herd behaviour) sustain booms. High valuation ratios such as the cyclically adjusted P/E (CAPE) predict lower long-run returns, though they are poor timing tools. Cultural and psychological anchors, not fundamentals alone, set prices, so popular narratives deserve suspicion when they justify unprecedented valuations.

---
title: Fooled by Randomness
author: Taleb
year: 2001
doc_type: book
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Many apparent successes reflect luck and survivorship; we see winners but not the graveyard. Rare, extreme events dominate outcomes, and strategies that earn steady small gains can carry hidden blow-up risk. Track records must be judged against the alternative histories that could have occurred.

---
title: Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth
author: Barber and Odean
year: 2000
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Using 66,465 US discount-brokerage households over 1991-1996, Barber and Odean find the most active traders earned about 11.4 percent a year net, against roughly 17.9 percent for the market and 16.4 percent for the average household. Gross returns were similar across turnover groups; trading costs and poor timing made the difference. Turnover above 250 percent a year destroyed returns. The authors attribute excessive trading to overconfidence. Frequent trading in small, illiquid stocks, where spreads and impact are higher, magnifies the damage.

---
title: Herd Behavior and Investment
author: Scharfstein and Stein
year: 1990
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Scharfstein and Stein model managers who care about their reputation for skill. Because a manager who follows the crowd and is wrong shares the blame (the sharing-the-blame effect), managers rationally mimic others' investment decisions and ignore their own private information. Herding can therefore be individually rational yet socially inefficient. It produces correlated errors, momentum in flows and prices that move away from fundamentals, and abrupt reversals when the herd changes direction. Consensus among professionals is weaker evidence than it looks.

---
title: The Disposition to Sell Winners Too Early and Ride Losers Too Long
author: Shefrin and Statman
year: 1985
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Shefrin and Statman combine prospect theory (losses hurt more than equal gains please), mental accounting (each position judged on its own), regret aversion and self-control problems to explain why investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long. Realising a loss means admitting a mistake, so investors defer it, even when tax rules favour selling losers. The disposition effect creates selling pressure on recent winners and a supply overhang on losers, which can slow price adjustment to news and help explain momentum.

title: Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap
author: Sullivan, Timmermann and White
year: 1999
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Sullivan, Timmermann and White test about 7,846 technical trading rules (filters, moving averages, support/resistance, channel breakouts, on-balance volume) on 100 years of Dow Jones data. Using White's Reality Check bootstrap to account for searching across many rules, they find the best rule appears significant in the original Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron sample, but its performance does not hold up in the subsequent out-of-sample decade. Any rule chosen because it worked historically must be judged against the whole universe of rules tried.

---
title: Survivorship Bias in Performance Studies
author: Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross
year: 1992
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross show that studying only funds or firms that survive creates spurious evidence of performance persistence and inflates average returns, because poor performers disappear from the sample. The bias grows with the volatility of the population and the length of the sample. Backtests run on today's list of listed stocks overstate what an investor could have earned, and the effect is largest for small, volatile and low-priced stocks, which delist or go bankrupt far more often.

---
title: The Black Swan
author: Taleb
year: 2007
doc_type: book
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Taleb defines black swans as rare, high-impact events that are explained only after the fact. Financial returns have fat tails, so models based on the normal distribution and standard deviation systematically understate the probability of extreme moves. Narrative fallacy and confirmation bias make people overconfident in forecasts. Positions that are leveraged, illiquid, concentrated or dependent on a single story are fragile; robustness comes from limiting exposure to ruin rather than from better prediction. Absence of evidence of risk is not evidence of absence.

---
title: Does the Stock Market Overreact?
author: De Bondt and Thaler
year: 1985
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Portfolios of prior three-to-five-year losers outperform prior winners by about 25 percent over the next three years, with the effect concentrated in January. Investors overreact to unexpected and dramatic news, so extreme long-run losers are often oversold and extreme winners overbought, consistent with long-horizon mean reversion.

---
title: The Limits of Arbitrage
author: Shleifer and Vishny
year: 1997
doc_type: paper
school: behavioral_risk
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Real-world arbitrage is done by specialised, capital-constrained professionals managing other people's money. When mispricing worsens, investors withdraw funds, forcing arbitrageurs to liquidate when opportunities are best. Arbitrage is therefore least effective in extreme circumstances and in volatile assets, so mispricing can persist and widen before it corrects.
