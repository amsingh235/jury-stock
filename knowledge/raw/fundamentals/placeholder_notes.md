---
title: Security Analysis
author: Graham and Dodd
year: 1934
doc_type: book
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Graham and Dodd define investment as an operation that, on thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Intrinsic value is a range justified by assets, earnings and dividends, not a precise figure. Earnings power should be averaged over a full business cycle (typically 7-10 years) to remove the distortion of peak or trough years. The margin of safety, buying well below conservative value, protects against analytical error and bad luck. Balance sheet strength, interest coverage and net current asset value are emphasised; analysts should be sceptical of reported earnings that are not backed by cash.

title: Berkshire Hathaway Shareholder Letters
author: Buffett
year: 1977
doc_type: letters
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Buffett shifts Graham's cigar-butt approach toward buying wonderful businesses at fair prices. Durable competitive advantages (moats) show up as high, stable returns on equity achieved without heavy leverage. Owner earnings, reported earnings plus depreciation minus maintenance capital expenditure, matter more than accounting profit. Management candour and rational capital allocation (buybacks only below intrinsic value) are quality signals. Buffett warns that turnarounds seldom turn and that a business requiring constant capital reinvestment to stand still destroys value.

---
title: Damodaran on Valuation (lecture notes)
author: Damodaran
year: 2012
doc_type: notes
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Value is a function of cash flows, growth and risk. Discounted cash flow valuation forces explicit assumptions about each. Relative valuation (P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA) is only meaningful against true peers with similar growth, risk and payout; a low multiple can simply reflect low growth or high risk. Every valuation should tell a story that ties to numbers, and the story must be possible, plausible and probable. For small emerging-market firms, country risk premia and illiquidity discounts apply.

title: Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information to Separate Winners from Losers
author: Piotroski
year: 2000
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Piotroski builds a nine-point F-score from profitability (positive ROA, positive operating cash flow, improving ROA, accruals: cash flow above net income), leverage and liquidity (falling leverage, improving current ratio, no new equity issuance) and operating efficiency (improving gross margin and asset turnover). Among high book-to-market stocks, high F-score firms outperform low F-score firms by about 23 percent a year in the US sample. The effect is strongest in small, thinly followed firms, which is relevant for low-priced stocks.

title: The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium
author: Novy-Marx
year: 2013
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Gross profits scaled by assets predict returns about as well as book-to-market. Profitable firms earn higher average returns despite having higher valuation ratios, so profitability works as a quality dimension of value. Controlling for profitability strengthens the value premium. Gross profit is a cleaner measure of economic productivity than net income because it sits above discretionary spending lines.

title: Do Stock Prices Fully Reflect Information in Accruals and Cash Flows about Future Earnings?
author: Sloan
year: 1996
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Earnings driven by accruals are less persistent than earnings driven by cash flows, but investors fixate on headline earnings. Firms with high accruals subsequently underperform and low-accrual firms outperform. A large gap between net income and operating cash flow is therefore a red flag for earnings quality and possible manipulation.

title: Financial Ratios, Discriminant Analysis and the Prediction of Corporate Bankruptcy
author: Altman
year: 1968
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. The Z-score combines working capital/total assets, retained earnings/total assets, EBIT/total assets, market value of equity/total liabilities and sales/total assets. In the original model, Z below 1.81 signals distress and above 2.99 signals safety, with a grey zone between. Variants exist for private and non-manufacturing firms. Distress risk is a key filter for low-priced stocks.

---
title: Separating Winners from Losers among Low Book-to-Market Stocks
author: Mohanram
year: 2005
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Mohanram's G-score applies a Piotroski-style signal set to growth stocks: profitability relative to industry, earnings and cash flow stability, and conservative accounting such as high research and advertising intensity. High G-score glamour stocks outperform low G-score ones, showing that fundamentals separate good from bad growth.

---
title: Contrarian Investment, Extrapolation, and Risk
author: Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny
year: 1994
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Value strategies outperform because investors extrapolate past growth too far into the future, overpricing glamour stocks and underpricing value stocks. The value premium does not appear to be explained by higher fundamental risk: value stocks did not underperform in bad states. This supports mean reversion in growth expectations.

---
title: The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns
author: Fama and French
year: 1992
doc_type: paper
school: value_quality
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Size and book-to-market together capture much of the cross-section of average returns, while market beta has little explanatory power on its own. Small firms and high book-to-market firms earn higher average returns. Fama and French interpret these as risk factors, while behavioural authors interpret them as mispricing.
