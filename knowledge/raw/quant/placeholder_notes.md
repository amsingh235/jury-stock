---
title: Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers
author: Jegadeesh and Titman
year: 1993
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Stocks with the highest returns over the past 3 to 12 months continue to outperform past losers over the following 3 to 12 months, roughly 1 percent a month. Part of the profit reverses after a year. The standard implementation, 12-1 momentum, skips the most recent month to avoid short-term reversal.

---
title: Value and Momentum Everywhere
author: Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen
year: 2013
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Value and momentum premia exist across individual stocks in several countries, equity indices, bonds, currencies and commodities. The two are negatively correlated, so a combination is far more stable than either alone. Common global factors link value and momentum returns across asset classes, with liquidity risk playing a role.

---
title: Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds
author: Fama and French
year: 1993
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. The three-factor model explains stock returns with the market excess return, SMB (small minus big) and HML (high minus low book-to-market). Factor loadings, not characteristics alone, determine expected return in their framework. The model became the benchmark for evaluating anomalies.

---
title: On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance
author: Carhart
year: 1997
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Adding a momentum factor (UMD) to the three-factor model explains most apparent persistence in mutual fund returns. Hot-hand fund performance is largely momentum exposure plus expenses, not skill. The four-factor model is standard for performance attribution.

---
title: Is Momentum Really Momentum?
author: Novy-Marx
year: 2012
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Novy-Marx shows that momentum profits are driven mainly by intermediate-horizon past returns, from twelve to seven months before portfolio formation, rather than the most recent six months. Strategies based on returns from months 12-7 outperform those based on months 6-2 across US stocks, industries and international markets. Momentum therefore looks less like simple trend continuation and more like an echo, which challenges behavioural stories that rely on gradual diffusion of recent news.

---
title: Fads, Martingales, and Market Efficiency
author: Lehmann
year: 1990
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Weekly and monthly returns show reversal: last period's losers tend to outperform last period's winners over the next short interval. The effect reflects liquidity provision and overreaction, and is partly consumed by transaction costs, especially in illiquid stocks.

---
title: The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing
author: George and Hwang
year: 2004
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. Nearness to the 52-week high predicts future returns better than past returns themselves. Investors anchor on the 52-week high and underreact to good news when price approaches it, so stocks near their highs keep drifting upward and do not reverse in the long run.

---
title: Time Series Momentum
author: Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen
year: 2012
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. An asset's own past 12-month excess return predicts its future return across futures markets: trends persist for about a year and partially reverse afterward. Time-series momentum performs well in extreme markets and is distinct from cross-sectional momentum.

---
title: Illiquidity and Stock Returns: Cross-Section and Time-Series Effects
author: Amihud
year: 2002
doc_type: paper
school: statistical
---
PLACEHOLDER knowledge note. The Amihud illiquidity measure, average absolute daily return divided by daily traded value, captures price impact. Illiquid stocks earn higher expected returns as compensation, and rising market illiquidity lowers contemporaneous prices. For small, low-priced stocks, liquidity both raises expected returns and raises the cost and risk of trading them.
